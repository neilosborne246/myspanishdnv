Best banks in Spain for expats — what digital nomads actually need

Opening a Spanish bank account is one of the first practical tasks after arriving on your Digital Nomad Visa. This guide covers every option — from digital bridging tools to traditional Spanish banks — so you can get sorted quickly.

By the My Spanish DNV team

Published: July 2026 Updated: regularly Read time: 9 min Category: Banking & Finance

Getting a Spanish bank account sorted quickly after you arrive is not just convenient — for DNV holders, it is practically essential. Rent, utilities, Social Security contributions, and your Spanish mobile contract all require a domestic IBAN. If you delay, you will find daily life frustratingly difficult. This guide tells you exactly what to do and which banks to consider.

The good news is that the Spanish banking sector is well developed, genuinely competitive, and increasingly digital-friendly. The less good news is that paperwork still plays a significant role, and the process differs depending on whether you have received your NIE yet. Here is the full picture.

Resident vs non-resident bank accounts in Spain

Spanish banks offer two distinct account types for foreign nationals: resident accounts and non-resident accounts. Understanding the difference matters because it affects both functionality and cost.

Non-resident accounts (cuenta de no residente)

A non-resident account can be opened with a passport alone — you do not need your NIE. This makes it accessible immediately on or after arrival. However, non-resident accounts typically come with higher maintenance fees, more restricted services, and annual tax reporting obligations (banks must report non-resident account holders to the Agencia Tributaria). They are a short-term solution only.

Resident accounts (cuenta de residente)

Once you have your NIE — which DNV holders receive as part of their TIE card — you can open a full resident account. These accounts offer lower or zero fees, full access to mortgages and loans, direct debit facilities at domestic rates, and all the standard services you would expect from a current account. This is the account you want for your life in Spain.

The practical timeline for DNV holders

Open a non-resident account or use Revolut/Wise immediately on arrival → collect your NIE and TIE → upgrade to a full resident account. Most DNV holders complete this transition within 30–60 days of arriving in Spain.

Documents needed to open a Spanish bank account

The documents required vary by account type and bank, but you should prepare the following:

  • Passport — valid and original (not a photocopy)
  • NIE — for resident accounts; your TIE card serves as proof
  • Proof of address in Spain — rental contract, utility bill, or empadronamiento certificate
  • Proof of income or employment — employment contract, recent payslips, or DNV approval letter
  • Spanish tax identification — your NIE number also functions as your NIF for tax purposes

Some banks will ask for all of the above; others are more flexible. CaixaBank and ING Spain are generally considered the most efficient at processing expat account applications. Sabadell has historically been very welcoming to British expats in particular, with English-speaking staff in many branches across popular expat areas.

Digital bridging tools — Revolut, N26, and Wise

Before your NIE arrives and before you can open a full resident account, you need something to receive money and pay for everyday expenses. Digital wallets and e-money accounts fill this gap well.

Revolut

Revolut is extremely popular among expats arriving in Spain. It offers a multi-currency account with a physical and virtual card, interbank exchange rates with no markup on weekdays, and a Spanish-language app. You can receive salary payments, pay by card virtually anywhere, and send money internationally at near-zero cost. Its IBAN is Lithuanian, which means it will not work for all Spanish direct debits — but as a day-to-day spending card it is excellent.

Wise (formerly TransferWise)

Wise is particularly strong for receiving income in one currency and spending in another. If your salary is paid in pounds or dollars and you are spending in euros, Wise's conversion rates and low fees make it the most cost-effective option. Like Revolut, the IBAN is not Spanish (Wise issues Belgian IBANs for euro accounts), so it works better as a bridging tool than a permanent solution.

N26

N26 is a fully licensed German bank that operates across the EU. It issues a genuine IBAN and a Mastercard debit card, with a genuinely strong mobile app. For EU citizens it is an excellent digital-first option. Non-EU nationals may face more limitations with the account opening process, but it is worth exploring.

Revolut and Wise IBANs will not work for everything

Spanish landlords, utility companies, and Social Security direct debits typically require a Spanish IBAN beginning with ES. A Lithuanian or Belgian IBAN will be rejected by some automated systems. Do not rely on Revolut or Wise as your permanent banking solution in Spain — open a proper Spanish account as soon as you have your NIE.

Santander

Santander is a Spanish bank that will be familiar to British expats — it has a significant UK presence. Its Spanish branches are widespread, its app is well-regarded, and its international transfer capabilities are among the best of the traditional banks. The Cuenta 1|2|3 current account offers cashback on utility bills, which is genuinely useful for expats with Spanish direct debits. Branch staff in tourist and expat areas often speak English.

BBVA

BBVA has invested heavily in digital banking and its app is consistently rated among the best in Europe. The Cuenta Online is a zero-fee current account with no minimum balance requirement and a straightforward online opening process. BBVA is popular with younger digital nomads who prefer to manage everything via their phone. English-language support is available through the app and online chat.

CaixaBank

CaixaBank is Spain's largest retail bank by branch network, which makes it the most accessible option if you live outside a major city. The Cuenta Día a Día is their standard current account with reasonable fees. CaixaBank also has one of Spain's strongest mortgage offerings for residents, which is relevant for DNV holders who go on to purchase property after their first year.

Sabadell

Sabadell built its expat reputation in the Costa Blanca and Costa del Sol regions, where it established a dedicated expat banking service with English-speaking staff. Its Cuenta Expansión is popular with foreign residents. If you are living along Spain's Mediterranean coast, Sabadell is worth a visit specifically because of its experience dealing with non-Spanish customers and the practical knowledge of its staff in those areas.

ING Spain

ING Spain operates as a digital-first bank with no branch network. Its Cuenta Nómina is a zero-fee current account with no maintenance charges, no minimum balance, and free ATM withdrawals across Spain when you meet a modest monthly income deposit threshold. The online and app experience is excellent and widely available in English. For digital nomads who do not need branch access, ING Spain is frequently the top recommendation.

How to open a resident account after your DNV is approved

Once you have arrived in Spain, collected your TIE card (which carries your NIE), and registered at your local town hall (empadronamiento), you are ready to open a full resident account. The process is straightforward:

  1. Choose your bank — based on the comparison above and your location in Spain
  2. Gather your documents — passport, TIE card (with NIE), empadronamiento certificate, rental contract or proof of address, employment contract or proof of income
  3. Visit a branch or apply online — ING Spain and BBVA allow fully online applications; others require an initial branch visit
  4. Complete the anti-money laundering check — all Spanish banks are required to conduct this; you will be asked about the source of your funds
  5. Receive your IBAN — typically within 24–72 hours of approval; your debit card follows within 5–10 business days
  6. Set up your direct debits — rent, utilities, Social Security contributions, and any other recurring payments

Why a Spanish IBAN matters from day one

A Spanish IBAN (beginning with ES) is not merely convenient — it is a practical requirement for life in Spain. Here is why it matters specifically for DNV holders:

  • Rent payments — virtually all Spanish landlords require direct debit from a Spanish IBAN; paying by international transfer adds friction and is often refused outright
  • Utility bills — electricity (Endesa, Iberdrola), gas, water, and internet providers all collect by direct debit from a Spanish account
  • Social Security contributions — if you are employed, your employer handles this; if you are autónomo, you pay quarterly Social Security contributions by direct debit from your Spanish account
  • Spanish salary payments — if you eventually take on a Spanish employer or clients paying in Spain, a Spanish IBAN simplifies payments significantly
  • Mortgage applications — if you intend to buy property, a Spanish bank relationship is a prerequisite for a Spanish mortgage
💡

DNV holders and Social Security — the 30-day window

As a DNV holder, your employer must register you with Spanish Social Security. This registration — and the subsequent Social Security contributions — are processed via Spanish banking systems. Having your Spanish bank account operational within the first 30 days of arrival ensures this process runs smoothly and without delays.

Banking fees and what to watch for

Spanish bank fees have reduced significantly over the past decade, driven partly by competition from digital banks. However, some charges remain common and are worth understanding before you commit to a bank:

  • Account maintenance fees — typically €5–12 per month, often waived if you receive a salary directly into the account
  • ATM withdrawal fees — using another bank's ATM can attract fees of €1–2 per withdrawal; ING Spain waives these
  • International transfer fees — SEPA transfers within the EU are generally free or very low cost; transfers outside the EU carry fees
  • Currency exchange — traditional banks charge poor exchange rates; use Wise for non-euro conversions
  • Overdraft fees — Spanish banks charge significant interest on arranged and unarranged overdrafts; keep your account in credit
