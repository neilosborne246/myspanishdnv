Living in Spain

Cost of living in Spain 2026 — what expats actually spend

Spain is significantly cheaper than the UK, Germany, and the Netherlands for an equivalent lifestyle. But the numbers vary dramatically by city. Here is the category-by-category breakdown, city by city, that you actually need to plan your move.

By the My Spanish DNV team

Published: July 2026 Updated: regularly Read time: 10 min Category: Living in Spain

One of the most frequently asked questions from people considering the Spain Digital Nomad Visa is a simple one: "Can I actually afford to live there?" The honest answer, for most people coming from the UK, Germany, the Netherlands, or the United States, is: yes — and usually more comfortably than you expect.

Spain occupies a useful middle ground in the European cost-of-living spectrum. It is significantly cheaper than Western European countries like the UK, Germany, and Scandinavia for an equivalent quality of life, and considerably more expensive than Eastern Europe or Latin America. Within Spain, the range is also wide — Madrid and Barcelona are genuinely expensive cities, while provincial capitals and smaller cities offer extraordinary quality of life at a fraction of the price.

This article gives you the actual numbers, city by city and category by category, so you can build a realistic budget rather than relying on generalisations.

Rent — the biggest variable

Rent is, by far, the biggest driver of cost-of-living differences between Spanish cities. Grocery prices, restaurant meals, and utilities are broadly similar across Spain. Rent is not.

The rental market in Spain has tightened significantly in recent years, particularly in Madrid, Barcelona, and popular coastal cities. Supply constraints, tourism demand, and the growth of short-term rental platforms have all contributed to rising rents. The figures above reflect 2026 market conditions — budget at the higher end of each range for well-located, good-quality properties.

Food and groceries

Spain's food costs are a genuine pleasure point for expats arriving from the UK or northern Europe. Groceries at Spanish supermarkets — Mercadona (the largest and most popular), Lidl, Carrefour, DIA, and Alcampo — are consistently cheaper than UK equivalents, and the quality of fresh produce, meat, and seafood is high.

A typical monthly grocery bill for a single person spending sensibly at Mercadona or Lidl runs to €250–350. A couple cooking at home most nights typically spends €350–500 per month on groceries.

Key staples that tend to surprise UK arrivals with their prices:

  • Olive oil (500ml, good quality): €4–7
  • Fresh bread (baguette-style): €0.30–0.80
  • Fresh chicken (1kg): €4–6
  • Tomatoes (1kg, fresh): €1.50–3
  • Bottle of decent table wine: €3–8
  • Bottle of beer (supermarket): €0.50–0.90

Eating out

Spain's restaurant culture is genuinely accessible at all budget levels. The menú del día is one of the great practical institutions of Spanish life — a three-course set lunch menu including a drink (wine, beer, or water), typically offered Monday to Friday, that gives you a proper sit-down restaurant meal for €10–15 at most establishments. In smaller cities and towns you can find them for €8–12. This makes eating out for lunch extremely affordable as a daily habit rather than a special occasion.

Evening dining is more expensive — a sit-down dinner for two at a mid-range restaurant runs to €40–70 including wine. A coffee (café con leche or cortado) costs €1.20–1.80 at most bars and cafés, compared to the £4–5 you might pay in a UK coffee shop.

Utilities and internet

Service Monthly cost (approx.)
Electricity, water, gas (1-bed flat) €80–120
Fibre broadband (300Mbps) €30–50
Mobile phone plan (unlimited data) €15–35

Spanish fibre broadband is excellent and extensively deployed — Spain has one of the highest rates of fibre-to-the-home penetration in Europe. Providers including Movistar, Vodafone, Orange, and MásMóvil compete aggressively on price, and 600Mbps–1Gbps symmetrical fibre packages are widely available for €40–60 per month. This matters for remote workers: Spain's internet infrastructure is genuinely world-class.

Electricity costs in Spain were elevated in 2022–23 following the energy crisis but have moderated. Average monthly electricity bills for a typical one-bedroom flat run to €50–80. Gas bills depend heavily on whether the property has gas central heating — many Spanish apartments use electric systems or split air conditioning units rather than gas, particularly in the south.

Transport

Spain's public transport infrastructure in major cities is excellent and very affordable:

  • Madrid Metro: monthly transport pass €54.60 (zone A, all modes)
  • Barcelona: T-Casual 10-trip card €11.35 (zone 1); most residents buy several per month
  • Valencia, Seville, Malaga: monthly bus/metro pass approximately €40–55
  • Intercity rail (Renfe AVE high-speed): Madrid–Barcelona from €29–70 depending on booking in advance; Madrid–Seville from €20–55

Owning a car in Spain is cheaper than in the UK. Fuel is lower in price than the UK (typically €1.65–1.85 per litre for unleaded in 2026). Car insurance premiums are generally lower. MOT equivalent (ITV) is less expensive and less frequent. Parking in most Spanish cities outside Madrid and Barcelona is easier and cheaper than UK equivalents.

Healthcare

Private health insurance is a requirement for DNV applicants and covers most DNV holders during their initial years in Spain. The cost depends on age and coverage level:

  • Under 40: €60–100/month
  • 40–55: €90–150/month
  • 55–65: €130–200/month
  • Over 65: €180–280/month

Major providers include Sanitas, Adeslas, Asisa, and Cigna. These policies typically include GP visits, specialist consultations, basic hospital treatment, and emergency cover. Dental and vision require separate add-ons.

For couples, shared fixed costs (rent, utilities, internet) make the per-person cost substantially lower than doubling a single-person budget.

The DNV income requirement in context

The Spain Digital Nomad Visa requires demonstrating monthly remote income of at least €2,849 per month (200% of Spain's monthly minimum wage). This figure is specifically chosen as the DNV's minimum threshold — it is not an arbitrary number.

In practical terms, €2,849 per month is very comfortably liveable in cities like Valencia, Seville, Malaga, Bilbao, Granada, and essentially any smaller Spanish city. In Madrid or Barcelona, it requires more careful budgeting — particularly on rent — but is entirely viable if you choose your neighbourhood thoughtfully.

Many DNV applicants earn significantly more than this minimum and find that Spain offers a dramatic quality-of-life upgrade compared to what the same income buys in London, Amsterdam, or Berlin.

💡

The location effect

Someone earning €4,000/month remote working from Seville is living a materially better lifestyle than the same person earning £4,000/month in London — lower rent, better weather, excellent food, slower pace, and lower taxes if structured correctly. This is the core proposition of the DNV for UK nationals.
