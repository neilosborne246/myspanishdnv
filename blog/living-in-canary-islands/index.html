Where to Live in Spain

Living in the Canary Islands — year-round warmth off the African coast

Spanish territory in the Atlantic, 1,700km south of Madrid, in the same time zone as the UK. The Canary Islands combine year-round warmth with full EU residency rights and a growing community of remote workers who have decided this is their base.

By the My Spanish DNV team

Published: July 2026 Updated: regularly Read time: 10 min Category: Where to Live in Spain

The Canary Islands are not in the Mediterranean. This surprises some people. The archipelago — eight main islands and several smaller ones — sits in the Atlantic Ocean off the northwest coast of Africa, roughly level with southern Morocco. At approximately 1,700km south of mainland Spain and 28°N latitude, the islands occupy a climatic sweet spot that produces consistently warm, mild weather year-round: typically 18–22°C in winter and 24–28°C in summer, with very little rain outside the northern slopes of the higher islands.

For DNV holders and remote workers, the Canary Islands offer a combination of advantages that is genuinely difficult to find elsewhere: Spanish territory (meaning full residency rights, no Schengen tourist cap, access to Spanish healthcare and infrastructure), year-round warmth, and — crucially — the same time zone as the United Kingdom. That last point matters more than most people initially appreciate.

The time zone advantage

UTC+0
Canary Islands time in winter — same as the UK, one hour behind mainland Spain

The Canary Islands operate on Western European Time — UTC+0 in winter and UTC+1 in summer. This is identical to the United Kingdom. For British remote workers, this means working with UK-based clients and colleagues with zero time zone friction — the same hours, the same working day, no awkward early or late calls to accommodate a time difference.

For remote workers serving North American clients, the UTC+0 base also gives better overlap with US East Coast hours than mainland Spain's UTC+1 position. A worker in Las Palmas on UTC+0 has an identical time zone relationship with New York as a worker in London — roughly five hours ahead of East Coast US time. From mainland Spain at UTC+1, that same overlap is six hours, which begins to limit the usable overlap window.

This time zone advantage is one of the most underappreciated arguments for the Canary Islands as a base for UK and US-focused remote workers.

The islands — a guide to each

Gran Canaria

Gran Canaria is arguably the best all-round choice for remote workers and longer-term expat residents. Its capital, Las Palmas de Gran Canaria, is a genuine city — population around 380,000 — with a university, major shopping centres, a significant cultural scene, excellent healthcare, and infrastructure that would not embarrass a mid-size mainland European city. Las Canteras beach in Las Palmas is one of the finest urban beaches in the world: over 3km of sand protected by a natural reef, clean water, and a lively promenade with excellent restaurants and cafés.

The south of the island — Maspalomas, Playa del Inglés, Puerto Rico — is dominated by tourism and resorts but offers reliable sunshine even when the north is occasionally cloudy. The interior of the island contains dramatic volcanic landscapes, reservoirs, and historic Canarian towns.

Tenerife

The largest Canary Island and the one with the most diverse landscape — from the black sand beaches of the northeast to Mount Teide (3,715m, mainland Spain and island Spain's highest point, and a UNESCO World Heritage site) to the tourist-saturated south coast. Santa Cruz de Tenerife is the island capital and a substantial city. La Laguna — a UNESCO World Heritage historic city just inland — is home to the University of La Laguna and a growing young professional and remote worker population. The south (Los Cristianos, Las Américas) is heavily touristic.

Lanzarote

The most dramatically volcanic of the main islands — the artist and architect César Manrique shaped much of its built environment, creating an island where architecture and volcanic landscape coexist in unusual harmony. Puerto del Carmen is the main tourist and expat hub. Lanzarote is drier and more arid than Gran Canaria or Tenerife, which appeals to those who want guaranteed sunshine. The island is smaller and quieter, with a distinct character — excellent for those who want beauty and calm over urban amenities.

Fuerteventura

Fuerteventura has the best beaches in the Canaries — and arguably some of the best in Europe. The island's long, wide, white-sand beaches on the west and east coasts are extraordinary. It is also the windiest island, making it a world-class destination for windsurfing and kitesurfing. The island is less developed and quieter than Gran Canaria or Tenerife, and suits those who prioritise natural environment over urban amenities. Puerto del Rosario is the capital; Corralejo in the north is the main expat hub.

La Palma

The greenest and most lush of the main islands, with dense laurisilva (laurel forest) covering its slopes. La Palma is the least touristed of the western islands and has a very different character — quieter, more local, with a strong sense of authentic Canarian life. The island is currently rebuilding after the 2021 Cumbre Vieja volcanic eruption, which destroyed several thousand homes in the southwest. The rest of the island was unaffected and remains beautiful. For those seeking genuine tranquillity away from tourist infrastructure, La Palma rewards the choice.

La Gomera, El Hierro

The smallest and least populated of the main western islands, both of which offer extraordinary natural beauty but very limited infrastructure. El Hierro runs almost entirely on renewable energy and has an ecological commitment that attracts a particular kind of resident. La Gomera is famous for its silbo gomero — the whistled language used historically across the island's valleys — and its dramatic laurisilva forest. Neither island suits remote workers who require strong connectivity or urban amenities.

One notable feature of the Canary Islands tax regime: the islands apply IGIC (Impuesto General Indirecto Canario) at a standard rate of 7%, compared to mainland Spain's IVA of 21%. This makes a range of goods and services meaningfully cheaper, from electronics and appliances to restaurant meals. The difference adds up across a year.

💡

Lower indirect taxes — a permanent advantage

The Canary Islands' IGIC at 7% versus mainland IVA at 21% is a structural feature of the islands' fiscal autonomy within Spain. It is not a temporary measure. Everything you buy locally — from furniture to food to services — benefits from this lower rate.

Healthcare in the Canary Islands

Healthcare provision in the Canary Islands is delivered through the Servicio Canario de la Salud (SCS), the regional health authority. The two principal public hospitals are Hospital Universitario Gran Canaria Dr. Negrín in Las Palmas (Gran Canaria) and Hospital Universitario de Canarias in La Laguna (Tenerife). Both are large, modern facilities with a full range of specialist departments.

Private healthcare is available on both Gran Canaria and Tenerife through groups including Quirónsalud and HM Hospitales, with English-speaking staff and shorter waiting times than the public system. On the smaller islands, private provision is limited and for serious conditions you may need to travel to Gran Canaria or Tenerife.

The DNV in the Canary Islands

The Spain Digital Nomad Visa is fully valid in the Canary Islands. There is no restriction on living in the Canary Islands as a DNV holder — you are simply a Spanish resident choosing to live in a particular part of Spanish territory.

The combination of factors that makes the Canary Islands appealing to DNV holders is significant:

  • Year-round warmth without the summer extremes of mainland Spain (Seville can reach 45°C in summer; Las Palmas never gets anywhere near this)
  • UK-aligned time zone for those working with British clients or employers
  • Lower cost of living than Madrid, Barcelona, or Mallorca
  • Full Spanish residency rights — no 90-day Schengen tourist limitation
  • Strong internet infrastructure in all main cities
  • Growing co-working and digital nomad community, particularly in Las Palmas

The DNV requires demonstrating remote income of at least €2,849 per month from non-Spanish sources (at least 80% of total income), along with qualifying private health insurance and clean criminal record certificates from your country of residence and any country you have lived in during the preceding five years.
