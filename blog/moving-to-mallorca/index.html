Where to Live in Spain

Moving to Mallorca — island living in the Balearics

Mallorca is far more than a summer holiday destination. Palma de Mallorca is a genuine, world-class European city with excellent infrastructure, year-round warmth, and a quality of life that draws expats from across the globe. Here is what you actually need to know.

By the My Spanish DNV team

Published: July 2026 Updated: regularly Read time: 9 min Category: Where to Live in Spain

Mallorca suffers from a reputation problem. For much of the UK and Germany it is primarily known as a package holiday destination — Magaluf, all-inclusive resorts, airport queues in August. That reputation is not entirely undeserved, but it represents perhaps 10% of what Mallorca actually is. The other 90% — Palma's magnificent Gothic cathedral, the limestone mountains of the Serra de Tramuntana (a UNESCO World Heritage site), the rural interior with its ancient stone fincas and almond groves, and the island's 300km of coastline ranging from hidden coves to sweeping bays — is what draws a very different kind of resident.

Mallorca has a large, established, and economically diverse expat community. British residents have been here for generations. Germans are so numerous in the northwest that local businesses operate in German as a matter of course. Scandinavians, Dutch, and increasingly Americans and Australians have discovered the island. The infrastructure that serves this community — international schools, English-speaking healthcare, specialist legal and financial services for foreigners — is sophisticated and well-developed.

Palma de Mallorca — the anchor

The vast majority of expats who live year-round in Mallorca base themselves in Palma or within a short drive of it. This is not simply convenience — it is because Palma is genuinely exceptional. The old city (Casc Antic) is one of the most beautiful historic centres in the Mediterranean, with the Catedral-Basílica de Santa Maria dominating the seafront and Arab Baths, medieval palaces, and Renaissance courtyards folded into the narrow streets around it.

Palma has a full range of shops, restaurants, hospitals, universities, theatres, and sports facilities. The airport — Palma de Mallorca Airport (PMI) — is one of the busiest in Spain, with direct flights to cities across Europe year-round (not only in summer), and good connections to Madrid and Barcelona for onward international travel.

The city is easily walkable in its central areas, and the bus network covers the wider municipality reasonably well. For getting around the island and particularly for reaching more rural properties, a car is effectively essential.

Where to live — neighbourhoods and areas

Palma centre — Old Town and Santa Catalina

Living in the heart of Palma puts you within walking distance of the cathedral, the best restaurants, the seafront promenade, and the city's cultural life. The Casc Antic has beautiful but often awkwardly configured apartments in historic buildings. Santa Catalina, the former fishing district just west of the centre, has become one of Palma's most fashionable neighbourhoods — younger demographic, excellent restaurants, and a lively street atmosphere.

Son Vida

The most exclusive address on the island. Son Vida sits on a hill above Palma with panoramic views across the bay. This is where you will find luxury villas with pools, golf courses, and a very private, wealthy residential atmosphere. Properties here are among the most expensive in Spain — villas start at several million euros. It suits those who want space, privacy, and proximity to Palma without being in the city itself.

Portals Nous and Portals Vells

South-west of Palma, the Portals area is one of Mallorca's most upmarket coastal zones — home to Puerto Portals marina, luxury yachts, and a strong concentration of high-end restaurants and shops. Apartments and villas here range from €500,000 to several million euros. The demographic skews British and international, with a polished, resort-town atmosphere.

Port Andratx

The jewel of Mallorca's southwest coast, Port Andratx is one of the most sought-after addresses in the Mediterranean — full stop. Set around a beautiful natural harbour with dramatic mountain scenery, it attracts an extremely affluent international community. Properties are very expensive: villas with sea views command €2 million and upwards, with exceptional properties reaching far higher. Day-to-day convenience is limited — it is a beautiful but relatively quiet town, particularly outside the summer months.

Sóller

Nestled in a valley in the northwestern Serra de Tramuntana, Sóller is one of the most charming towns in Mallorca. The old wooden tram that runs from Sóller to its port is an attraction in itself. Sóller has a significant British and German expat community, excellent local restaurants, a weekly market, and a slower pace of life than Palma. The mountain scenery is spectacular. It is about 30 minutes from Palma through a tunnel, which makes commuting straightforward if needed.

Alcúdia

In the north of the island, Alcúdia is a genuinely Mallorcan walled town with a strong local character alongside a substantial expat and tourist presence. The beach at Playa de Alcúdia is one of the finest on the island — wide, shallow, and 4km long. For expats who want to feel more integrated into authentic Mallorcan life rather than an international bubble, Alcúdia rewards that choice.

Property purchase prices reflect the island's demand. An apartment in Palma typically ranges from €250,000 to €500,000 depending on size, location, and condition. Rural fincas in the interior vary enormously — from €400,000 for something requiring significant work to several million euros for a restored property with land. Southwest coast villas in areas like Port Andratx and Portals Nous start at €1 million and frequently exceed €5 million for larger properties with sea views.

Internet and remote working infrastructure

For DNV holders and remote workers, connectivity is critical, and Mallorca's situation is broadly positive. Palma and the main towns have full fibre-to-the-home (FTTH) coverage through Movistar, Vodafone, and Orange, with gigabit speeds available. Co-working spaces have expanded significantly in Palma over recent years — there are now a dozen or more professional co-working venues in the city, catering specifically to the growing digital nomad and remote working population.

In rural areas and smaller villages, connectivity is variable. Before renting or buying a property outside a main town, always verify the specific address's broadband availability using the provider's own coverage checker, and test with a temporary mobile data connection if possible. Mobile coverage (4G and 5G) is strong across most of the island, which provides a backup option.

Healthcare in Mallorca

Mallorca's healthcare provision has improved substantially over the past decade, driven in part by the demands of its large international resident population.

On the public side, Hospital Son Espases is the island's main public hospital — a modern, large-capacity facility on the outskirts of Palma with a full range of departments and specialist services. It opened in 2010 and is the primary public healthcare reference centre for the Balearic Islands.

Private healthcare is well-developed and caters extensively to international patients. Clínica Rotger and Clínica Palmaplanas are the principal private hospitals in Palma, both with English-speaking staff and a comprehensive range of specialist services. USP Clínica Palmaplanas also offers a strong international patient service. Most private healthcare procedures are covered by standard international health insurance, and waiting times at private facilities are short compared to the public system.

Seasons and island life

Mallorca's year divides meaningfully into two distinct phases. From May to October the island is at or near full capacity — the peak tourist season brings millions of visitors, prices rise across the board, beaches are busy, and the social and restaurant scene is at its most vibrant. This is Mallorca at its most energetic.

From November to April, the island quietens dramatically. Some seasonal businesses close. Beaches are empty. Temperatures remain mild — typically 12–18°C — and the island reveals a very different character: quieter, more authentically local, considerably cheaper, and in many ways more pleasant for residents. The almond blossom in February, when the interior of the island turns white and pink, is one of the most beautiful natural events in Spain. Many long-term expat residents prefer the island in winter precisely because it returns to something closer to what it was before mass tourism.

The DNV in Mallorca

The Spain Digital Nomad Visa is an excellent fit for Mallorca. The combination of excellent infrastructure (for those based in or near Palma), year-round warmth, high quality of life, and a large existing international community makes the island particularly appealing to remote workers who want something more than a mainland city experience.

The DNV requires demonstrating remote income of at least €2,849 per month, with at least 80% from non-Spanish sources, plus qualifying private health insurance and clean criminal record certificates. The application can be made from your home country at the nearest Spanish consulate, or in-country via the UGE (Unidad de Grandes Empresas) if you are already in Spain with a valid residence status.
