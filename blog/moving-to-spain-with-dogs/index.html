Moving to Spain with dogs — the rules every pet owner needs to know

Since Brexit, moving your dog from the UK to Spain involves more paperwork than many people expect. An EU Animal Health Certificate, microchip, rabies vaccination, and tapeworm treatment are all required. Here's exactly what you need to do — and when.

By the My Spanish DNV team

Published: July 2026 Updated: regularly Read time: 10 min Category: Practical Guides

Spain is a wonderfully dog-friendly country. Dogs are welcome in many restaurants, shops, and public spaces. Spanish cities are generally well set up for dogs, with good access to parks, beaches (at certain times of year), and outdoor areas. It is no surprise that many families moving to Spain on the Digital Nomad Visa (DNV) want to bring their dogs with them.

The paperwork, however, has become considerably more complex since Brexit. The UK is now classified as a non-EU third country for the purposes of pet travel — which means the simpler rules that once applied between EU member states no longer apply when moving a dog from Britain to Spain. This guide sets out everything you need to know for a smooth crossing.

Note that these rules apply to dogs specifically. Cats and ferrets have similar — though not identical — requirements. This article focuses on dogs.

EU rules vs non-EU rules — why Brexit changed everything

Before Brexit, dogs could travel between the UK and EU countries using a simple EU pet passport issued by a UK vet. That passport documented microchip, rabies vaccination, and (where applicable) tapeworm treatment — and that was broadly sufficient for travel within the EU/EEA zone.

From 1 January 2021, the UK became a third country under EU veterinary rules. Pet passports issued in the UK are no longer accepted for entry into EU member states, including Spain. Instead, dogs from the UK (and from all other non-EU countries such as the US, Australia, Canada, and South Africa) must now use an EU Animal Health Certificate (AHC) each time they travel to an EU country.

This is a significant practical change for UK dog owners relocating to Spain, because the AHC is not a one-time document — it is a certificate issued for a specific journey, valid for a limited period, and must be obtained from an official vet every time you travel from a non-EU country into the EU.

What your dog needs to enter Spain from the UK

The requirements are cumulative — your dog must satisfy all of them, and the order and timing of certain steps matters:

1. Microchip

Your dog must be fitted with an ISO 11784/11785 compliant microchip (15-digit). Crucially, the microchip must be implanted before the rabies vaccination is administered. If the vaccination was given before the microchip, it is not considered valid for travel purposes and the vaccination must be repeated after the chip is in place. If your dog was microchipped years ago in the UK and vaccinated afterwards in the correct order, you are fine — but verify the dates on your records.

2. Rabies vaccination

Your dog must be vaccinated against rabies. The vaccination must have been administered at least 21 days before the date of entry into Spain. Dogs that have had a continuous series of valid rabies vaccinations (booster given before the previous one expired) do not need to wait 21 days after a booster — only after a primary vaccination. Keep all vaccination records: the certificate must show the date of vaccination, the batch number of the vaccine, and the vet's details.

3. Tapeworm treatment (dogs from the UK only)

Dogs travelling from Great Britain must receive a tapeworm treatment (Echinococcus multilocularis) from a registered vet. The treatment must be administered no less than 24 hours and no more than 5 days before your arrival in Spain. This is a rolling window tied to your specific date of entry — it cannot be done too far in advance. Your vet must record the treatment in the AHC or in a separate certificate.

This requirement does not apply to dogs travelling from Northern Ireland under the specific Northern Ireland rules, nor to dogs originating from countries where Echinococcus multilocularis is not present. For most UK dog owners, this treatment is a non-negotiable step.

4. EU Animal Health Certificate (AHC)

The AHC is the central document. It must be issued by an Official Veterinarian (OV) — a government-authorised vet, not just any vet — in the country of origin. In the UK, OVs are registered with the Animal and Plant Health Agency (APHA). You will need to find a vet clinic with an OV or have your records sent to one.

The AHC must be issued no more than 10 days before entry into the EU. Once issued, it is valid for 10 days for entry, and then — once in the EU — for travel within the EU for 4 months or until the date the rabies vaccination expires (whichever is sooner).

The timing sequence matters

Microchip first (must predate the vaccination) → rabies vaccination (minimum 21 days before entry for primary vaccination) → tapeworm treatment (no more than 5 days and no less than 24 hours before arrival) → AHC issued (no more than 10 days before arrival). Plan these steps carefully — getting the order or timing wrong means your dog cannot travel.

Approved entry points into Spain — Border Inspection Posts

Dogs entering Spain from a non-EU country must pass through a designated Border Inspection Post (BIP) — a specific port or airport authorised to carry out documentary and physical checks on live animals. You cannot fly into any Spanish airport and simply collect your dog — it must be a BIP.

The approved BIPs in Spain for live animals (Category A) currently include:

  • Barcelona El Prat Airport
  • Bilbao Airport
  • Las Palmas Airport (Gran Canaria)
  • Madrid Barajas Airport
  • Seville Airport
  • Valencia Airport

If you are planning to relocate to an area not served by one of these airports, you may need to fly into the nearest BIP and then travel overland to your destination, or use a specialist pet transport company that can manage the border inspection process on your behalf.

Always check BIP status before booking flights

BIP designations can change. Before booking flights for yourself and your dog, verify that your intended airport remains a designated BIP with current authorisation to process dogs arriving from non-EU countries. The TRACES NT system (maintained by the European Commission) holds current BIP information.

Breed restrictions in Spain — Potentially Dangerous Dogs

Spain has national legislation — Real Decreto 287/2002 — governing Potentially Dangerous Dogs (Perros Potencialmente Peligrosos — PPP). Owners of dogs on the PPP list face additional requirements across all Spanish municipalities.

The breeds currently listed under national PPP legislation include:

  • Pit Bull Terrier
  • American Staffordshire Terrier
  • Rottweiler
  • Dobermann
  • Argentine Dogo (Dogo Argentino)
  • Brazilian Fila (Fila Brasileiro)
  • Tosa Inu
  • Akita Inu

Mixed-breed dogs with characteristics of these breeds may also be subject to PPP rules depending on interpretation by local authorities. If your dog is of a listed breed or mixed heritage involving a listed breed, take advice from your local ayuntamiento (town hall) once you arrive.

PPP requirements for owners

Owners of PPP breeds must hold a Licencia PPP (PPP licence) issued by their local municipality. To obtain the licence you generally need: proof of no criminal convictions for violent offences, a medical and psychological fitness certificate, third-party liability insurance for your dog (with a minimum coverage level set by each municipality), and a DNA certificate identifying your dog. PPP dogs must be kept on a lead and wearing a muzzle in public spaces in most municipalities. They cannot be left unsupervised in places accessible to the public.

Note that some Spanish regions and municipalities have additional local restrictions that go beyond the national legislation. Some communities ban certain breeds entirely. Research the specific rules for your municipality — particularly if you are moving to Catalonia, Madrid, or the Canary Islands, which have their own regulatory frameworks.

Registering your dog in Spain after arrival

Once in Spain, there are several registration steps to complete:

Vet microchip scan and regional registry

Take your dog to a registered vet in Spain. They will scan the microchip to confirm it is working and register your dog on the regional animal registry (called RIVIA in some regions, or equivalent). This is the foundation of your dog's legal identity in Spain.

Municipal registration (licencia municipal)

Most Spanish municipalities require dog owners to register their dogs with the local ayuntamiento (town hall) and obtain an annual dog licence (licencia municipal de tenencia de animales). There is usually a small annual fee — typically €10–30 depending on the municipality. Some cities also require proof of third-party liability insurance as part of the licence process, regardless of breed.

Annual rabies vaccination

Annual rabies vaccination is legally required in most Spanish regions. Your vet will maintain a vaccination record and provide a certificate. Keep this document — you may need it for the municipal licence renewal, and you will certainly need it for any future travel back to the UK or to other non-EU countries.

Pet health insurance in Spain

Veterinary costs in Spain are generally lower than in the UK or US, but they can still be substantial for serious illness or surgery. Pet health insurance is widely available in Spain from both Spanish insurers (such as Mapfre and Allianz) and international pet insurance companies with Spain coverage. Policies typically cover accident and illness treatment, with optional extras for preventive care and dental treatment.

If you currently hold UK pet insurance, contact your insurer before the move to understand whether your policy continues to provide cover in Spain and for how long — most UK pet policies are designed for UK-based animals and will not cover a dog permanently resident in Spain.

Dog-friendly Spain — what to expect day to day

Beyond the bureaucracy, Spain is genuinely one of the best countries in Europe for dog owners. Dogs are commonly seen in cafés, restaurants with outdoor terraces, and shops in most cities. Larger cities — Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia, Seville — have excellent networks of parks and designated off-lead areas. The climate means year-round outdoor activity is possible in most of Spain.

Some beaches in Spain allow dogs in designated sections — usually outside the main summer season (broadly September to June) — and some municipalities maintain year-round dog beaches. Check local ordinances for the specific beaches and parks near your intended home.

Apartment living with a dog is very common in Spanish cities. Many landlords accept dogs, although it is worth confirming this explicitly before signing a rental contract. Furnished properties may have restrictions on larger breeds. If you are buying property, there are no restrictions on dog ownership.
