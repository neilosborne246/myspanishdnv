Real estate lawyer in Spain — how to find one you can trust

The notary who witnesses your signature in Spain does not protect you — and neither does the agent's in-house contact. Here's what an independent property lawyer actually does, what to ask before you engage one, and how to verify their credentials.

By the My Spanish DNV team

Published: July 2026 Updated: regularly Read time: 10 min Category: Property & Legal

The single most important decision a foreign buyer makes in the Spanish property process is not which property to buy or which mortgage to take. It is whether to engage an independent property lawyer — and if so, which one. Get this right and the transaction is protected. Get it wrong and the consequences can be severe, expensive, and sometimes irreversible.

This guide explains exactly what a real estate lawyer does in Spain, why the notary does not substitute for legal representation, how to find and vet a lawyer you can genuinely trust, and what questions to ask before signing a fee agreement.

The notary and the lawyer — a critical distinction

The most widespread misconception among foreign buyers in Spain is this: "We signed at the notary, so everything must be in order." This conflates two entirely different functions.

A notario (notary) in Spain is a public official — a highly qualified civil servant appointed by the state. Their role is to authenticate legal documents, verify the identities of the parties, certify that signatures are genuine, and register the transaction with the land registry. The notary ensures the transaction is formally valid. They do not represent the buyer. They do not carry out due diligence on the buyer's behalf. They will not tell you if the property has an illegal extension, an outstanding mortgage, an unpaid IBI bill, or a missing building licence.

A real estate lawyer (abogado inmobiliario) acts exclusively for you. They are your advocate, your investigator, and your protector throughout the transaction. Their work happens largely before you sign anything — in the due diligence phase — and continues through to completion and beyond.

Both are necessary. The notary provides legal formality; the lawyer provides legal protection. Treating the notary's presence as a substitute for independent legal advice is one of the most costly misconceptions in Spanish property buying.

What a real estate lawyer does — the full scope

A competent property lawyer in Spain will carry out the following for you:

Pre-contract due diligence

  • Obtain and analyse the Nota Simple from the Registro de la Propiedad — confirming ownership, charges, and property description
  • Compare the Nota Simple with the catastro certificate — identifying any discrepancies in size, boundaries, or classification
  • Check with the local ayuntamiento (town hall) for the urban planning classification of the land and any restrictions, pending planning orders, or compulsory purchase risks
  • Verify that all buildings and structures on the site have licencias de obras (building permits) and that they match the registered descriptions
  • Obtain the certificado de habitabilidad (habitability certificate) or licencia de primera ocupación (first occupation licence)
  • Check for unpaid IBI (property tax) via the local council
  • Obtain a certificado de la comunidad confirming no community fees are outstanding
  • Verify the seller's identity and legal authority to sell (particularly relevant in inheritance cases or where a company is selling)

Contract review and negotiation

  • Review and negotiate the arras contract (preliminary purchase agreement) — adding protective clauses for mortgage conditions, survey results, and due diligence findings
  • Review any private purchase agreement between the parties
  • Advise on the appropriate deposit structure and timing

Completion and post-completion

  • Review the notarial deed before signing
  • Attend or represent you (via Power of Attorney) at the notary
  • Handle payment of the transfer tax (ITP or IVA depending on property type) within the statutory deadline
  • Register the completed deed at the Registro de la Propiedad
  • Set up utilities in your name and arrange direct debits for IBI and community fees

Abogado versus asesor — know the difference

Spain has a distinction that matters enormously in practice:

Abogado — a fully qualified lawyer, required to have completed a law degree plus a postgraduate Master in Abogacía, and registered with their local Colegio de Abogados (Bar Association). They are subject to professional discipline and are required to hold professional indemnity insurance.

Asesor (or asesor jurídico/legal consultant) — a title with no regulated qualification requirement in Spain. Anyone can describe themselves as an asesor. They may be highly competent — many are qualified lawyers working informally — but they may also be completely unqualified, and they are not required to hold indemnity insurance.

Always verify the Colegio de Abogados registration

Before engaging any lawyer for a Spanish property transaction, ask for their Colegio de Abogados registration number and the bar association they belong to. Verify this at abogacia.es — Spain's national bar association directory. An unregistered "legal adviser" who turns out to be uninsured and unqualified is not a risk worth taking on a six-figure transaction.

How to find an independent English-speaking lawyer

There are three reliable routes:

1. The Spanish Bar Association directory

The Consejo General de la Abogacía Española maintains a searchable directory of all registered Spanish lawyers at abogacia.es. You can search by location, speciality, and language. This is the definitive verification source — not only for finding lawyers but for confirming that someone claiming to be a lawyer is actually registered.

2. Your country's consulate

The British, US, Australian, and Canadian consulates in Spain maintain lists of English-speaking lawyers known to them. These lists are not endorsements, but they are a reasonable starting point for identifying lawyers with experience serving foreign national clients.

3. Personal recommendations — with conditions

Recommendations from friends or colleagues who have completed successful Spanish property purchases are valuable. Be clear about one rule: the recommendation must not come from your estate agent, developer, or any party with an interest in the transaction proceeding. A lawyer recommended by the seller's agent is not independent — regardless of how many successful transactions they have processed together.

What to ask before engaging a property lawyer

Questions to ask any prospective property lawyer in Spain
  • Are you registered with a Colegio de Abogados? What is your registration number?
  • Do you hold professional indemnity insurance? For what amount?
  • Are you completely independent — do you have any commercial relationship with this agent or developer?
  • How many transactions of this type have you completed in the last year?
  • What specifically is included in your fee — what searches, checks, and services?
  • What is NOT included — are post-completion tasks (tax payment, registration) extra?
  • Will you personally handle the file, or will it be passed to a junior?
  • How do you communicate — and what is your typical response time?
  • Can you provide a written scope of work and fee agreement before I engage you?

What a real estate lawyer costs in Spain

The standard fee for a real estate lawyer in Spain is approximately 1% of the purchase price plus IVA (currently 21%). On a €250,000 property this is €2,500 plus IVA = €3,025. On a €500,000 property it is €5,000 plus IVA = €6,050.

Many lawyers set a minimum fee — commonly €1,500–2,500 plus IVA — regardless of the purchase price. For higher-value or complex transactions (off-plan, rural property, commercial components, title issues), fees are typically negotiated on a case-by-case basis.

The fee for post-completion work — tax payment, land registry registration, utility transfers — may be included in the overall fee or charged separately; clarify this before engaging.

For context: on a property purchase at €300,000, a property lawyer costs approximately €3,630 including IVA. The total buying cost stack — including transfer tax, notary, registry, and legal fees — runs to approximately €36,000–45,000 for a non-resident buyer. The lawyer's fee is roughly 8–10% of the total buying costs, and is the portion that provides the legal protection for the entire transaction.

Remote purchasing and Power of Attorney

Many foreign buyers cannot be present in Spain for every stage of the transaction — or cannot attend the notary completion appointment in particular. This is entirely manageable via a Poder Notarial — a notarised Power of Attorney.

A Power of Attorney authorises your lawyer to sign documents on your behalf in Spain. It can be granted in one of three ways:

  • At a Spanish notary while you are visiting Spain — the most common approach
  • At the Spanish consulate in your home country — appropriate if you cannot travel to Spain at all
  • Before a local notary in your home country, with an apostille attached — an apostille is the international certification that makes the document valid in Spain under the Hague Convention

Your lawyer will prepare the draft Power of Attorney text specifying exactly what they are authorised to do on your behalf. Read it carefully — the scope of authority should be sufficient for the transaction but not broader than necessary.

Real estate lawyers in Spain — FAQ

In Spain, the notary (notario) is a civil servant who authenticates legal documents — including property deeds — and certifies the identities and signatures of the parties. The notary does not represent either buyer or seller, does not carry out legal due diligence, and is not responsible for protecting your interests. A real estate lawyer (abogado), by contrast, acts exclusively for you. They conduct due diligence before exchange: checking the Nota Simple, planning permissions, community debts, IBI payments, building permits, and habitability certificates. They review the preliminary contract (arras) for protective clauses. They accompany you (or attend on your behalf) at the notary. The notary's presence at completion does not make an independent lawyer unnecessary.
A real estate lawyer in Spain typically charges around 1% of the purchase price plus IVA (currently 21%) for a standard residential purchase. On a €300,000 property, that is approximately €3,000 plus IVA = €3,630. Some lawyers work to fixed fees, particularly for lower-value transactions — minimum fees of €1,500–2,500 plus IVA are common. Complex transactions (off-plan, rural property, commercial elements, or significant legal problems discovered during due diligence) typically cost more. The fee is one of the best-value expenditures in the whole process: a lawyer who identifies a debt, a planning breach, or a missing building licence at the due diligence stage can save you far more than their fee.
Yes, there is no legal requirement to have a lawyer when buying property in Spain. Many people do so without one — and a proportion run into serious, expensive problems as a result. The risks are real: illegal buildings, inherited debts, unregistered property, missing building licences, planning restrictions, disputed ownership, and fraudulent sellers are all documented problems in the Spanish property market. For a foreign buyer unfamiliar with the Spanish legal system, the language, and the administrative checks required, buying without an independent lawyer is a significant risk. The cost of a lawyer — roughly 1% of the purchase price — is modest compared with the potential consequences of proceeding unadvised.
The most reliable ways to find an English-speaking property lawyer in Spain are: (1) the official Law Society directory at abogacia.es — Spain's Bar Association website, where you can search by location and speciality; (2) your country's embassy or consulate in Spain maintains a list of registered English-speaking lawyers; (3) personal recommendations from people who have completed successful purchases, not from your estate agent. Ask any prospective lawyer for their Colegio de Abogados registration number — this is Spain's mandatory professional registration for qualified lawyers — and verify it on the Bar Association website. Avoid anyone who presents themselves as a 'legal adviser' or 'consultant' without an abogado qualification, as they may not be qualified or insured.
A Power of Attorney (Poder Notarial) is a legal document that authorises another person — typically your lawyer — to act on your behalf in Spain. If you cannot attend the notary signing in person (because you are abroad, or the completion date is inconvenient), a Power of Attorney allows your lawyer to sign the completion deed on your behalf. Powers of Attorney for Spanish property transactions must be notarised — either at a Spanish notary while you are in Spain, or at a Spanish consulate in your home country, or before a local notary with an apostille. Your lawyer will prepare the specific text required. Remote purchasing via Power of Attorney is common and entirely legally effective.
