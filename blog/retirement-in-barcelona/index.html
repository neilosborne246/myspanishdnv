Retiring in Spain

Retiring in Barcelona — world-class city, premium price

Barcelona is the city that makes people abandon their plans and never leave. Architecture, beach, food, culture, and an international energy that few cities anywhere can match. Here is what you actually need to know before you move.

By the My Spanish DNV team

Published: July 2026 Updated: regularly Read time: 9 min Category: Retiring in Spain

Barcelona sits near the top of almost every list of best places to retire abroad — and for good reason. It is a fully functioning, cosmopolitan, European capital-grade city with a beach. It has one of Europe's best metro systems, world-renowned architecture, Michelin-starred restaurants alongside excellent everyday food, and an international community of over 300,000 foreign residents who have concluded the same thing you are probably concluding right now: Barcelona is extraordinary.

It is also the most expensive city in Spain after Madrid, and the gap with the rest of the country is significant. A one-bedroom flat in Eixample will cost you what a two-bedroom house costs in Valencia. That is the trade-off, and it is one that most Barcelona expats decide is worth making. This guide helps you make that decision with clear, accurate information rather than wishful thinking.

Why Barcelona? The genuine case

Barcelona's appeal is not difficult to articulate, but it is worth being specific rather than vague about what makes it exceptional.

Architecture and urban design

The Eixample grid — designed by Ildefons Cerdà in the 1850s — is one of the most successful pieces of urban planning in history. Wide, tree-lined boulevards with chamfered corners, ground-floor colonnades, and interior courtyards create a neighbourhood that is genuinely pleasant to walk through at any time of day. Gaudí's legacy — the Sagrada Família, Casa Batlló, Park Güell, Casa Milà (La Pedrera) — gives the city an architectural vocabulary you will not find anywhere else on earth. After six months in Barcelona, you stop noticing these things consciously, but they continue to shape the quality of your daily environment.

The beach

Barceloneta beach is right in the city. This is not common. Most European capital cities are either not on the coast or have beaches that require a significant journey. In Barcelona, you can walk from the centre to the Mediterranean in twenty minutes. The water is warm from May to October. The beaches stretch north and south — Barceloneta itself is the most central and therefore the most crowded in summer, but Bogatell, Mar Bella, and Nova Mar Bella to the north offer more space.

Food and restaurants

Barcelona's food scene operates at a level that surprises even those who arrive with high expectations. The Boqueria market on La Rambla is the famous one, but the Mercat de Sant Antoni and the Mercat de l'Abaceria in Gràcia are better for everyday shopping and less orientated to tourists. The city has a deep culture of eating out — the menú del día (set lunch menu) offers three courses with wine for €12–18 at hundreds of restaurants across the city, which makes the cost of eating out far lower than it appears on a first visit.

International airport and connectivity

Barcelona El Prat (BCN) is one of Europe's major hub airports with direct flights to destinations across North America, South America, the Middle East, and Asia, as well as comprehensive European coverage. For expats who need to travel frequently — to visit family, maintain business relationships, or simply explore — Barcelona's connectivity is a significant practical advantage.

The rental figure is the biggest variable. If you are prepared to live in Gràcia, Poblenou, or the less central parts of Sant Martí, you can find one-bedroom flats in the €1,100–1,500 range. If you want a two-bedroom flat in Eixample or Sarrià-Sant Gervasi, budget €2,000–3,000. Property prices for purchase run from €350,000 for a modest one-bedroom flat in a less central area to €800,000 and above for well-located larger apartments.

Best neighbourhoods for expats

Eixample

The default choice for most new arrivals, and for good reason. Eixample sits at the centre of the city with excellent metro and bus connections in every direction. The neighbourhood is divided into Eixample Esquerra (left) and Eixample Dreta (right), with the latter being slightly more prestigious and expensive. Wide pavements, a huge density of restaurants, gyms, supermarkets, language schools, and medical clinics make it very practical for day-to-day life. The downside is that it feels more urban and less characterful than some other neighbourhoods — you are living in a city, and that is what it feels like.

Sarrià-Sant Gervasi

Barcelona's wealthiest residential district climbs the lower slopes of the Collserola hills above the city. Quieter, greener, and more family-orientated than Eixample, with good international schools nearby (the American School of Barcelona, the British School of Barcelona, and others are within reach). Properties are larger and more expensive. The FGC rail line connects Sarrià and Gràcia to the city centre quickly. If you are coming from a suburban lifestyle and want to maintain something of that feel within Barcelona, this is the area to look at.

Gràcia

Gràcia was a separate municipality before being absorbed into Barcelona in 1897 and retains a distinct village-within-the-city atmosphere. Narrow streets, independent shops, excellent local restaurants, and a strong community identity make it particularly appealing to expats who want to integrate rather than remain in an expatriate bubble. The Festival de Gràcia in August — where residents decorate entire streets in themed installations — is one of Barcelona's most genuine local celebrations. Slightly cheaper than Eixample for comparable properties.

Barceloneta

Living directly on the beach sounds idyllic and, in practice, has significant drawbacks. Barceloneta is extremely noisy from May to October, heavily touristed, and the apartments are generally small (the neighbourhood was originally built as fishermen's housing). It suits people who genuinely want beach proximity as the primary consideration and can live with the noise and footfall. Outside summer it is considerably quieter and more pleasant.

Poblenou

Barcelona's former industrial district has been transforming for two decades into a creative and technology hub, anchored by the @22 innovation district. Poblenou offers newer, larger apartments at slightly lower prices than Eixample, good beach access, and a less touristy atmosphere. It appeals to remote workers and younger professionals in particular, and is increasingly well-served by restaurants and amenities as the area continues to develop.

Healthcare in Barcelona

Barcelona's healthcare is genuinely excellent at both public and private levels.

On the public side, Hospital Clínic de Barcelona is one of Spain's leading research and clinical hospitals, internationally recognised for transplant medicine, cardiology, and infectious disease. Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau — a UNESCO World Heritage site for its remarkable Modernista architecture — operates as a full-service public hospital. Both are within reach of the city centre.

Private healthcare is well-developed. Clínica Quirón Barcelona is part of Spain's largest private hospital group, with English-speaking specialists, fast appointment times, and comprehensive services. Teknon in Sarrià is another highly regarded private clinic popular with international residents. Private health insurance for expats in Barcelona typically costs €80–150 per month for adults under 55, rising to €150–250 for those over 60. This is a mandatory requirement if you are not yet entitled to access the public system.

Accessing public healthcare as a new arrival

As a new resident in Barcelona, you will typically need private health insurance until you are entitled to access the public system. Employed workers and autónomos who contribute to Seguridad Social gain access automatically. For others, the Convenio Especial — a voluntary contribution of around €60/month — provides a route to public healthcare coverage.

The Catalan language consideration

Catalan is the co-official language of Catalonia alongside Spanish (Castilian) and is genuinely used in daily life in Barcelona — particularly in schools, local government, some businesses, and among Catalans speaking to other Catalans. You will see Catalan signage everywhere, hear it in conversations around you, and encounter it in official documents.

For practical daily life, Spanish is completely sufficient. Every Catalan speaker also speaks Spanish fluently, and in international areas of the city you will frequently hear English, French, Italian, and German as well. Healthcare staff speak Spanish, government offices work in both languages, and banks operate in Spanish without issue.

Where Catalan matters is in integration. Barcelona has a more distinct regional identity than most other Spanish cities — Catalans are proud of their language and culture, and making a genuine effort to learn at least basic Catalan (even just bon dia, gràcies, per favor) is received with warmth rather than just politeness. If you plan to be in Barcelona long-term and want to build genuine local friendships rather than remaining in an expat circle, learning Catalan over time is worthwhile.

Barcelona's expat community

With over 300,000 registered foreign residents — and many more who have not formally registered — Barcelona has one of the largest and most established expat communities in Europe. This is both a strength and a trap. The strength is that you can arrive in Barcelona and immediately plug into organised sports leagues, language exchanges, professional networks, cultural events, and social groups where English is the common language. The trap is that you can live in Barcelona for years entirely within this international bubble without ever engaging with Spanish or Catalan culture or language at a meaningful level.

Barcelona rewards those who push past the bubble. The city's culture, its food, its festivals, its architecture, and its people are far richer than what is visible from the surface layer that serves international tourists and expats.

Visa options for living in Barcelona

Two main routes are relevant to non-EU nationals relocating to Barcelona:

The Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) suits retirees and those living off passive income, pensions, savings, or investments who do not intend to work. It requires demonstrating financial means — currently around €2,400 per month plus a proportion for any dependants — and qualifying private health insurance. The NLV does not permit working.

The Spain Digital Nomad Visa (DNV) is the correct route for those who are still working remotely for clients or employers based outside Spain. The DNV requires demonstrating active remote income of at least €2,849 per month, with at least 80% of that income coming from non-Spanish sources. It also requires private health insurance at the point of application. The DNV allows its holder to work remotely while living in Spain legally.

If you are semi-retired — perhaps still doing consultancy work or freelance projects — the DNV may be more appropriate than the NLV, as the NLV prohibits all working activity.
