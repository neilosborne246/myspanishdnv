Retiring in Spain

Retiring in Malaga — why the Costa del Sol's capital is booming

Malaga has transformed from a gateway airport city into one of Spain's most exciting places to live. 320 days of sun, a genuine cultural revival, excellent healthcare, and an international community that is growing fast.

By the My Spanish DNV team

Published: July 2026 Updated: regularly Read time: 12 min Category: Retiring in Spain

For most of the twentieth century, Malaga was seen as the Costa del Sol's airport — a place you passed through on your way to Marbella, Fuengirola, or Torremolinos. That reputation is now thoroughly outdated. Over the past decade, Malaga has emerged as one of Spain's most dynamic and desirable cities, attracting international residents, remote workers, tech companies, and retirees in growing numbers. Understanding why — and what it actually costs — is the purpose of this guide.

Why Malaga has boomed

Malaga's transformation is driven by several converging factors. The city's cultural infrastructure has expanded dramatically: the Picasso Museum (in Picasso's own birthplace) has been joined by the Carmen Thyssen Museum, a branch of the Paris Pompidou Centre, and a cluster of other world-class cultural institutions that together make Malaga's historic centre one of the most culturally rich in Andalusia.

The Málaga Tech Park (Parque Tecnológico de Andalucía, or PTA Málaga) has attracted significant technology investment, bringing an influx of younger, international professional residents who have in turn expanded the city's restaurant scene, cultural life, and expat community. This influx has benefited retirees too — more international residents means better English-language services, more diverse restaurants and shops, and a more internationally minded social atmosphere.

days of sunshine per year in Malaga — among the highest of any city in mainland Europe

The climate is outstanding: 320 days of sunshine per year, with mild winters that rarely drop below 10–12°C and warm, dry summers. The sea moderates temperatures compared to inland Andalusia, keeping Malaga more comfortable than Seville in peak summer. The international airport — Malaga–Costa del Sol — operates direct routes to dozens of European cities, making Malaga one of the best-connected cities in southern Spain for regular international travellers.

Malaga's neighbourhoods — where to live as a retiree

Historic centre (Centro Histórico)

Malaga's historic centre is undergoing continuous regeneration. The streets around the Cathedral, the Alcazaba fortress, and the Picasso Museum are now lined with excellent restaurants, wine bars, and boutique shops. Living in the centre puts you within walking distance of everything — museums, markets, transport, and the city beach (La Malagueta) is a 15-minute walk along the waterfront. Rents are higher than in residential suburbs, but the lifestyle is superb for retirees who want urban vibrancy on their doorstep.

La Malagueta

Malaga's main city beach neighbourhood sits immediately east of the port. The paseo marítimo (sea promenade) here is exceptional — wide, well-maintained, lined with restaurants and bars, and busy with residents year-round rather than just in summer. For retirees who want beach access without leaving the city, La Malagueta is the obvious choice.

Pedregalejo and El Palo

These two adjacent beach neighbourhoods, about 4–5 km east of the city centre, retain a genuinely local, authentic Spanish character that the touristified centre has partly lost. The chiringuitos (beach bars) here serve some of the finest espetos (sardines grilled over an open fire) in Spain. Both areas are popular with long-term expat residents who want a relaxed, neighbourhood atmosphere alongside the sea. Rents are moderately lower than the centre.

Miraflores and Teatinos

These quieter residential neighbourhoods west of the centre are popular with families and retirees who want calm, green surroundings with good transport links. They lack the urban buzz of the centre but offer larger properties at lower rents, and are well-served by Malaga's metro and bus network.

Cost of living in Malaga

Malaga sits at a moderate point on Spain's cost spectrum — more expensive than inland cities like Seville, Murcia, or Alicante, but significantly cheaper than Madrid or Barcelona. Rent for a one-bedroom apartment in a central or beachside neighbourhood runs approximately €800–1,200 per month. A comfortable two-bedroom apartment for a couple in a pleasant area costs €1,200–1,800 per month.

Beyond rent, day-to-day costs in Malaga are very reasonable. Eating out is excellent and affordable — the local tapas culture means that a glass of wine or beer typically comes with a small tapa at many bars, and a full restaurant meal for two with wine can be had for €30–50 in most neighbourhood restaurants. Fresh produce from the Mercado Central de Atarazanas is exceptional and affordable. Utilities run approximately €90–130 per month for a one-bedroom apartment.

Healthcare in Malaga

Malaga has an excellent healthcare infrastructure, both public and private. The public system is anchored by the Hospital Regional de Málaga — one of the largest hospitals in Andalusia, with a full range of specialist departments — and the Hospital Universitario Virgen de la Victoria. Both are modern, well-equipped facilities providing high-quality care.

For private healthcare, Malaga offers several strong options:

  • HC Málaga (Hospital del Carmen) — well-regarded private hospital with English-speaking staff
  • Hospital Quirónsalud Málaga — part of Spain's largest private hospital group, with excellent facilities and strong English language service
  • Hospital HM Málaga — newer private facility with modern equipment
  • Clínica Nuestra Señora de la Victoria — established private clinic popular with expat patients

Private health insurance for a retiree in their early 60s typically costs approximately €100–180 per month for comprehensive cover. Most private clinics in Malaga have staff who speak English, German, or other European languages, and the city's large expat population means the medical infrastructure is well-attuned to international patients.

Malaga's expat community

Malaga has one of the most diverse expat communities in southern Spain. British residents have been coming to the Costa del Sol since the 1960s, and there is a well-established British community with all the associated infrastructure — English-language newspapers, social clubs, British-style pubs, and professionals (doctors, solicitors, accountants) who work in English.

The community has grown significantly more international in recent years, with large numbers of German, Scandinavian, Dutch, and American residents joining the traditional British and Northern European cohort. This broadening makes Malaga a genuinely cosmopolitan city rather than a British enclave with Spanish weather.

Marbella — for those who want more

Marbella, approximately 55 km southwest of Malaga city, represents the higher end of Costa del Sol living. With its famous Golden Mile, Puerto Banús marina, and world-class restaurants and golf courses, Marbella attracts a wealthier international crowd. Property in central Marbella and on the Golden Mile is expensive — apartments from €400,000 upwards, with villas reaching many millions. For retirees with a generous budget who want access to the most luxurious infrastructure on the Costa del Sol, Marbella is compelling. For those on more modest budgets, Malaga city offers the vast majority of the same benefits at a lower cost.

Property prices in Malaga

Property prices in Malaga city have risen substantially over the past five years, driven by the city's growing desirability. In 2026, typical prices in Malaga city are:

  • One-bedroom apartment (city centre or near beach): approximately €200,000–350,000
  • Two-bedroom apartment (pleasant residential area): approximately €280,000–500,000
  • Larger family home or villa on the outskirts: €400,000 and upwards

Purchase costs in Spain add approximately 10–13% on top of the purchase price for taxes (typically 7–10% ITP on resale properties in Andalusia, plus notary, registry, and legal fees). A qualified Spanish property lawyer (separate from your estate agent) is essential — do not proceed with any purchase without independent legal advice.

Visa for retiring to Malaga

Non-EU nationals wishing to retire to Malaga have two principal visa options. Spain's Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) is the standard choice for retirees living entirely on passive income — pensions, investments, rental income. It requires demonstrating approximately €28,800 per year of income for the main applicant and prohibits paid work.

For retirees who are still generating income from remote consulting, part-time professional work, or a foreign employer, Spain's Digital Nomad Visa (DNV) is the appropriate route. The DNV requires a minimum monthly income of €2,849 (the 2026 figure) and permits remote working for non-Spanish clients or employers. It also provides potential access to Spain's Beckham Law flat 24% tax rate for qualifying employed workers.

Transport in Malaga

Malaga is exceptionally well-connected by Spanish and European standards. The city has a metro system (two lines connecting the city centre, the university, and the airport), an extensive bus network, and a tram line along the waterfront. The international airport is approximately 10 minutes from the city centre by metro — a remarkably convenient arrangement that makes arriving and departing from Malaga genuinely simple.

By rail, Malaga is connected to Madrid by high-speed AVE train in approximately 2.5 hours — making weekend trips to the capital easy. The cercanías (commuter rail) network connects Malaga with Fuengirola, Torremolinos, and other Costa del Sol towns, giving residents access to the whole western Costa without needing a car.

DNV holders in Malaga

Malaga is one of Spain's top choices for Digital Nomad Visa holders — the combination of excellent connectivity (fast fibre, coworking spaces, good airport), a large and diverse international community, and a stimulating lifestyle makes it ideal. The DNV income requirement of €2,849 per month provides a comfortable standard of living in Malaga, with surplus available for savings or lifestyle spending.
