Retiring in Spain

Retiring in Valencia — Spain's most liveable city for expats

300 days of sun, a beach 20 minutes from the city centre, world-class food, low costs, and a growing international community. Valencia keeps topping the rankings — here is why.

By the My Spanish DNV team

Published: July 2026 Updated: regularly Read time: 13 min Category: Retiring in Spain

Ask expats who have lived in multiple Spanish cities where they would choose to retire, and Valencia comes up more consistently than anywhere else. It is not the most glamorous or the most famous — Madrid has the prestige, Barcelona has the international cachet, and the Costa del Sol has the established expat infrastructure. But Valencia has something none of those places quite manages: the complete package at a price that makes sense.

This guide is about understanding what retiring in Valencia actually looks and feels like — which neighbourhoods, what it costs, where to get healthcare, what the property market offers, and which visa route you need to make it happen.

Why Valencia stands apart

Valencia is Spain's third-largest city, with a population of around 800,000 in the city itself and 1.5 million in the wider metropolitan area. It is large enough to offer everything a major city should — international airport, major hospitals, universities, cultural institutions, excellent restaurants, theatres, and museums — while remaining genuinely human in scale. Getting around Valencia is easy, the city is walkable, and the pace of life is unhurried in a way that contrasts sharply with Madrid or Barcelona.

The headline facts: 300 days of sunshine per year, a Mediterranean climate with hot dry summers and mild winters (January averages 10–12°C), a beach at Malvarrosa and El Cabanyal that is 20 minutes from the city centre by metro, and the Mercado Central — one of the world's great food markets — at the heart of the historic quarter. Valencia is the birthplace of paella and has arguably the richest food culture of any Spanish city, which is saying something considerable.

The cost of living is Valencia's most practical advantage. Rent for a one-bedroom apartment in a desirable central neighbourhood runs €700–1,000 per month — a fraction of what equivalent housing costs in London, Amsterdam, or the Nordic capitals. A single retiree can live comfortably in Valencia on €1,200–1,600 per month including rent. A couple on €1,800–2,500 per month will live well. These are not frugal budgets — they include eating out regularly, social activities, and a comfortable lifestyle.

Valencia's neighbourhoods — where retirees actually live

Ruzafa

Ruzafa (Russafa in Valencian) has become Valencia's most talked-about neighbourhood. Once working-class and somewhat neglected, it has transformed over the past decade into the city's most cosmopolitan district — packed with independent coffee shops, international restaurants (Japanese, Korean, Middle Eastern, Mexican, and everything in between), wine bars, boutique shops, and a thriving creative and professional community. It has the highest concentration of English-speaking residents and international expats in the city. Rent in Ruzafa is among the highest in Valencia — typically €850–1,100 for a one-bedroom — but it remains excellent value by any comparative standard.

El Carmen

El Carmen is Valencia's historic heart — the medieval quarter, with narrow streets, Roman-era walls, beautiful churches, and a bohemian character that draws artists, musicians, and culture-lovers. It has a lively nightlife scene that some retirees find excessive (particularly at weekends), but during the day and in the weekday evenings it is an extraordinary place to live. Rent is somewhat lower than Ruzafa.

Eixample

Valencia's Eixample (Ensanche) is the wide, Haussmann-style grid of boulevards south of the historic centre. It is quieter than Ruzafa, more residential in character, and well-served by public transport and amenities. Many longer-term expat residents — particularly those with families or who prefer a calmer neighbourhood atmosphere — settle in the Eixample. Rent is moderate.

Malvarrosa and El Cabanyal

These beachfront neighbourhoods, undergoing significant regeneration, are the obvious choice for retirees who want immediate beach access. Malvarrosa beach is one of the best urban beaches in Spain — wide, clean, and backed by a long paseo marítimo with restaurants and bars. El Cabanyal, the former fishing village behind the beach, has been undergoing regeneration and now combines authentic Valencian character with a growing creative community. Rents here are slightly lower than in Ruzafa or El Carmen.

Jesús and Patraix

These southern residential neighbourhoods offer genuine affordability — one-bedroom apartments can be found for €600–750 per month — alongside good transport connections and a local, authentic Spanish character. They lack the social scene of Ruzafa but are popular with budget-conscious expats who want a comfortable home base and easy access to the rest of the city.

Cost of living in Valencia — the full picture

Beyond rent, Valencia is consistently one of the most affordable cities in Western Europe for day-to-day living. The numbers for a typical single retiree in 2026:

  • Rent (1-bed, decent neighbourhood): €700–1,000 per month
  • Food and groceries: €250–350 per month (combining cooking at home and eating out 3–4 times a week)
  • Utilities (electricity, water, gas): €80–120 per month
  • Internet (fast fibre): €30–45 per month
  • Public transport pass: €20–40 per month (metro, bus, and tram all integrated)
  • Private health insurance: €80–160 per month (depending on age and cover)
  • Social activities, coffee, dining out: €150–300 per month

Total for a single person: approximately €1,310–2,015 per month. A comfortable mid-point is around €1,400–1,600 for most people. For a couple, add approximately €400–600 for accommodation upgrades plus proportional increases in food and social spending — a typical couple's total runs €1,800–2,500 per month.

Healthcare in Valencia

Valencia's healthcare provision is excellent. The public hospital system is anchored by the Hospital Universitari i Politècnic La Fe — one of the largest and most technically advanced hospitals in Spain, with a full range of specialist departments and internationally recognised research programmes. Other public hospitals include the Hospital Clínic Universitari and Hospital Doctor Peset.

For private healthcare, the main options in Valencia are:

  • Hospital Quirónsalud Valencia — part of Spain's largest private hospital group, with English-speaking staff and modern facilities
  • Hospital 9 de Octubre — well-regarded private hospital on the southern edge of the city
  • Clínica Benidorm (Ribera Salud) — private clinic group with several Valencia locations
  • Hospital HM Valencia — newer private facility with strong diagnostic capabilities

Private health insurance for a retiree in their early 60s in Valencia typically costs approximately €90–160 per month for comprehensive cover including hospital admission, specialist consultations, and emergency treatment. Valencia has a well-established network of English-speaking private GPs and specialists familiar with the needs of expat patients.

Property in Valencia

Valencia's property market has risen substantially over the past five years, driven by increasing international demand. In 2026, approximate prices in Valencia city are:

  • One-bedroom apartment (Ruzafa, El Carmen, Eixample): approximately €120,000–220,000
  • Two-bedroom apartment (central): approximately €180,000–350,000
  • Two-bedroom apartment or house (beachfront Malvarrosa): approximately €250,000–450,000
  • Larger three-bedroom apartment (central): approximately €300,000–600,000

These prices are still remarkably low by Western European standards — equivalent properties in London, Amsterdam, or Zürich cost three to five times as much. Buying in Valencia typically adds 10–12% in taxes and fees (ITP is 10% in the Valencian Community for resale properties, plus notary, registry, and legal costs). Using a qualified Spanish property lawyer independent from your estate agent is essential.

Valencia's language — Spanish and Valencian

Valencia is officially bilingual: Spanish (Castilian) and Valencian — a language closely related to Catalan — are both co-official in the Valencian Community. In practice, Spanish works for virtually all daily interactions, and the Valencian expat community is welcoming and accustomed to international residents. Some official communications and signage are in Valencian, but this rarely causes practical difficulty for Spanish speakers.

Learning Spanish will significantly enhance your experience of living in Valencia and is strongly recommended. Valencian, while interesting to learn, is not necessary for comfortable daily life.

Valencia's expat community

Valencia has a substantial and diverse international community. British, German, Dutch, American, and Australian expats are well-represented, along with large communities of Italian, French, and other European residents. The expat community is well-organised — there are numerous English-language social groups, sports clubs, language exchange events, and cultural organisations that make settling in straightforward.

The community has grown noticeably over the past five years as Valencia's reputation has spread among remote workers and younger retirees seeking alternatives to the Costa del Sol. This influx has brought more international restaurants, English-language services, and a richer social scene, while Valencia retains far more of its authentic Spanish character than heavily touristified coastal towns.

Climate and outdoor life

Valencia's climate is Mediterranean at its finest. Summers are hot and dry (July and August average 28–30°C by day, rarely exceeding 35°C — significantly cooler than inland Seville or Madrid). Winters are genuinely mild — January and February average 10–12°C with regular sunshine, and frost is extremely rare. Spring and autumn are near-perfect: warm, sunny, and comfortable.

Outdoor life in Valencia is exceptional. The Turia riverbed — converted into a 9-kilometre-long linear park after the river was rerouted in the 1960s — runs through the heart of the city, providing cycling paths, gardens, sports facilities, and the City of Arts and Sciences complex at its southern end. The beach is accessible year-round for walking and cycling even in winter. The mountains of the Valencian interior are within easy driving distance for hiking and skiing (Javalambre ski resort is approximately 90 minutes away).

Valencia as a DNV base

For retirees still generating income from remote work or a foreign employer, Valencia is one of the strongest bases in Spain for the Digital Nomad Visa. The city has excellent fibre broadband infrastructure (30–50 Mbps fibre packages for €30–45 per month), a well-developed coworking scene (with spaces including Wayco, Impact Hub Valencia, and Espai VERD among many others), and direct flight connections to London, Amsterdam, Frankfurt, Rome, and dozens of other European cities from Valencia Airport.

The DNV income requirement of €2,849 per month provides a very comfortable standard of living in Valencia — the surplus after all living costs allows for substantial savings, frequent travel, or lifestyle upgrades like a larger apartment or beachfront location.

Valencia and the DNV — the numbers work

A single DNV holder on the minimum income of €2,849/month in Valencia faces typical living costs of €1,300–1,600/month. That leaves €1,200–1,500 per month surplus — enough to save, travel extensively, or simply live at a higher standard. For those earning above the minimum, Valencia is transformatively affordable.

Which visa to retire in Valencia

For non-EU nationals, the two main routes are:

Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) — for retirees living entirely on passive income. Requires approximately €28,800 per year for the main applicant (plus €7,200 per additional dependent). Prohibits paid work. Private health insurance is a mandatory condition. This is the standard retirement visa for those living on pension, investment income, or savings.

Digital Nomad Visa (DNV) — for retirees who are still generating income from remote work, consulting, or a foreign employer. Requires a minimum of €2,849 per month (2026 figure). Permits remote working for non-Spanish clients and employers. Provides potential access to Spain's Beckham Law flat 24% tax rate for qualifying employed workers.

EU citizens can move to Valencia freely and register as residents without a visa, though they must complete the relevant administrative registration (empadronamiento and NIE/TIE processes) to establish legal residency.
